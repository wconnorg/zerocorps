## Why keep one

Memory is a poor record keeper. It remembers the big win and the painful loss, forgets the dull trades in between, and quietly edits your reasons after the fact. A trading journal is the written record of what you actually did, trade by trade, so that later you can learn from facts instead of impressions.

It does not need special software. A spreadsheet, a notebook or a note in Obsidian all work. What matters is that you record the same fields for every trade, without exception.

## The fields

| Field | What to write |
| --- | --- |
| Date and time | When you entered |
| Market and setup | The contract, and the setup's name and version |
| Direction | Long or short |
| Entry | The price, and which condition triggered it |
| Stop | The price at entry, and the risk in dollars (1R) |
| Target | The planned exit |
| Exit | The price, and the reason: stop, target, time or manual |
| Result | In R, and in dollars after commissions |
| Rules followed | Yes or no, and which rule was broken |
| Notes | Market context, and your state of mind |
| Screenshot | One at entry, one at exit |

The **result in R** makes trades comparable. The **rules followed** field is the most honest one in the table, and often the most useful.

## Record before the trade and after it

Split the entry into two moments.

**Before the trade**, write the setup, entry, stop, target, size and the reason. Do it before you know the outcome, because once you know it, memory bends toward it. A loss makes the entry "obviously" bad; a win makes a sloppy entry look clever.

**After the trade**, fill in the exit, the result, whether you followed your rules, your notes and the exit screenshot.

## The weekly review

Once a week, sit down with the journal and work through the same questions:

- How many trades, and what were the win rate, average win and average loss in R?
- What was the total in R, and the expectancy per trade?
- How many trades broke a rule, and which rule?
- What do the screenshots of the losing trades have in common? And the winners?
- What is the **one** thing to change or practise next week?

Keep **mistakes** separate from **losses**. A losing trade that followed every rule is not a mistake; it is the setup's normal cost. A winning trade that broke a rule is a mistake, even though it made money.

## The raw material for everything later

Every statistic in the Backtesting School, from win rate and expectancy to drawdown and losing streaks, is calculated from rows like these. If rows are missing, or the fields change from week to week, the numbers are wrong, and wrong numbers are worse than none because they feel trustworthy.

So record every trade, including the ones you would rather forget. Those are usually the ones with the most to teach.
