## Acceptance

A price is **accepted** when the market spends time there and trades volume there. You need both ingredients.

When a market moves to a new area and starts trading back and forth, with contracts changing hands at each price and new periods of time stacking up, it is accepting those prices. It has found a place where both buyers and sellers are willing to keep doing business, which is exactly how value forms.

Some traders define acceptance concretely, for example as two or more 30-minute periods at or beyond a level. There is no single official number. The exact definition is yours to write, and the Backtesting School shows you how to turn it into a rule.

## Rejection

A price is **rejected** when the market reaches it and leaves quickly, with little time spent and little volume traded.

Rejection leaves marks behind:

- **A tail**, a stretch of prices at a high or low that traded only briefly.
- **Excess**, the name for a clean end to an auction. Price went too far, the other side responded hard, and trade ended there.

A long tail at a session's low describes buyers stepping in strongly and refusing to let lower prices trade. A long tail at the high describes the same thing from sellers.

## A test versus a break

When price reaches an important level, such as yesterday's high or the edge of a value area, one of two things happens.

- **A test.** Price probes beyond the level and returns quickly. The level held, and the prices beyond it were rejected.
- **A break.** Price moves through the level and is accepted beyond it. Time and volume build on the other side.

In the first few minutes, a test and a break can look identical. The difference only shows as time passes, which is why a single candle closing beyond a level is not acceptance.

## Why time matters more than a wick

A wick shows that price reached a level. It does not show how long price stayed there or how much traded. A long wick on a 5-minute candle might be four minutes of steady trade at those prices, or ten seconds.

Timeframe matters too. A wick on a 5-minute chart may be a full candle body on a 1-minute chart. The candle chart alone cannot tell you whether a level was accepted or rejected.

## Reading it on the profile, not just the candles

Profiles, which you meet in the next chapter, show time and volume at each price directly. That makes acceptance and rejection visible in a way candles are not.

| | Acceptance | Rejection |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Time at the price | Many periods | Brief |
| Volume at the price | Builds up | Thin |
| Profile shape | Wide and rounded | Narrow, with a tail |
| How price moved | Slowly, back and forth | Fast, in one direction |

> [!tip] Wait for the evidence
> When price pushes beyond a level, the honest description at first is "testing". It becomes "accepted" or "rejected" only once time and volume have had a chance to show.

> [!check] Price pushes above yesterday's high, a candle closes above it, and within ten minutes price is back inside yesterday's range. What happened at that high?
> - [ ] Acceptance, because a candle closed above it
> - [x] A test that was rejected, because little time and volume built above the level
> - [ ] A break, because price traded above the level
> Acceptance needs time and volume at the new prices, and a brief close provides neither.
